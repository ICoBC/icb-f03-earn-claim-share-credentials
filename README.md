# Earn, Claim and Share Your Credentials

A practical guide for earners, from accepting a badge to using it in an application.

## About this course

**Course ID:** ICB-F03

**Who it is for:** Earners and learners, learning designers and teaching staff, and L&D and corporate academy leads. You need no prior knowledge.

**What you will be able to do.** After this course you will be able to:

1. Claim a credential and store it in a backpack or wallet of your choice.
2. Share a credential on a profile, CV or application and check how a recipient will see it.
3. Set privacy options and decide what to make public.
4. Describe what to do when a credential expires, is revoked or the issuer closes.

## What you received and why it matters

### The credential you hold

You complete a course, a workshop or a project. A few days later an email arrives: "You have been awarded a badge." What is in that email, and what should you do with it?

A digital credential is a record that an issuer says you achieved something. The issuer is the organisation that awards it, for example a university, a training provider, a company academy or an association. The record is stored as data with a link to your name or email address, a description of the achievement, the date and the issuer's details. Many digital badges follow the Open Badges 3.0 specification. A broader record of learning, which can list several achievements together, can follow the Comprehensive Learner Record (CLR) 2.0 standard. Both are current specifications.

You are the earner. The credential is about you, and you decide what happens to it after you accept it. The ICoBC Credentialing Skills Framework calls this earner agency: you claim, store and share your own credentials and you make informed choices about privacy.

### What a credential contains

Most credentials show the same core information. You can find it on the badge page or in the details view of your backpack.

- **Name and description:** what the credential is called and what it says you did.
- **Criteria:** what you had to do to earn it.
- **Issuer:** who awarded it, with a link to their page.
- **Date issued:** and sometimes an expiry date.
- **Evidence:** optional work samples or links you or the issuer attached.
- **Verification information:** data that lets a recipient check that the issuer really awarded it.

Read these fields before you share anything. A recipient will judge your credential by what it claims, not by what you remember about the course.

### Why it matters to you

A credential helps people see what you did without asking you to explain it from scratch. It can support a job application, a promotion discussion, a request for credit at a college or a conversation with a mentor. It only does this when you can find it, share it and show that it is genuine.

There is also a practical side. Credentials can change after you receive them. An issuer may set an expiry date, withdraw (revoke) a credential that was awarded by mistake or that no longer meets its criteria, or close down. Knowing this from the start helps you keep your own records in order.

**Worked example: Amina, HR assistant in a corporate academy**

Amina works in the HR department of a mid-sized manufacturer. The company academy issues a badge "Safe handling of personal data (Ability)" after a workshop. She receives an email with a button "Accept your badge". Before she clicks, she reads the description and the criteria. The badge page says it was issued on 4 November 2026 and expires after 24 months (an illustrative period). She notes the expiry date in her calendar, because her manager will ask for a valid credential in an audit. She also sees that the issuer is the academy, not an external body. She will say this accurately when she mentions the badge to others.

**Practice activity: Read a credential record**

Open the sample credential your facilitator provides, or the demo badge in your sandbox. Do not claim it yet. Read the badge page and write down, in your own words:

1. The issuer and the date of issue.
2. The criteria in one sentence.
3. Whether it has an expiry date.
4. One thing you would check before sharing it.

Keep your notes. You will use them in the next modules.

Ask participants to swap notes in pairs and see whether they read the same criteria. Differences are a useful starting point to discuss why precise wording matters when you describe a credential to others. Allow about ten minutes.

### What can happen after you receive it

Three events can change the status of a credential, and Outcome 4 asks you to know what to do about each.

- **Expiry:** the credential has an end date. After that date a recipient will usually see it marked as expired. You can renew it if the issuer offers a refresher, or keep it on your record as evidence of past achievement.
- **Revocation:** the issuer withdraws the credential. A recipient who checks it will see that it is no longer valid. Contact the issuer to ask why and to find out whether it can be reissued.
- **Issuer closure:** the organisation stops operating. The hosted badge page may disappear. Keep a copy of the credential file and the issuer's contact details, and note the closure date.

You will return to these three cases in the module "Claiming and storing". First, check your understanding.

## Claiming and storing

### Claim your credential

Claiming means accepting a credential that an issuer has sent you, so that it becomes part of your own record. Until you claim it, the credential may sit unused in an email or on the issuer's platform.

A typical claim follows these steps:

1. Open the notification email and check that it comes from an issuer you recognise.
2. Click the claim link. Never enter a password on a page you reached through an unexpected link. If something looks wrong, go to the issuer's website directly.
3. Sign in or create an account if the platform asks you to.
4. Review the credential details: name, issuer, criteria, date.
5. Accept the credential.

If the email does not arrive, check your spam folder and ask the issuer to resend it. The credential may have been sent to a different email address than the one you expected.

### Backpacks and wallets

A backpack or wallet is a place where you keep your credentials under your own control. Some are hosted online by a platform, others run on your device. You can use one that your issuer recommends or one you choose yourself. A good choice has these features:

- It lets you import credentials from different issuers.
- It lets you export your credentials as files, so that you are never locked in.
- It shows verification status, expiry and revocation.
- It lets you set visibility for each credential.
- It states clearly how it uses your data.

Platform support for Open Badges 3.0 and CLR 2.0 is still uneven. Check whether a tool states that it has passed conformance certification for the version you need, and read the issuer's instructions for how to import credentials. <!-- VERIFY: current conformance certification programme names and status before publishing -->

### Wallets and the wider picture

Beyond badge backpacks, public bodies are building digital identity wallets. The European Digital Identity (EUDI) Wallet comes from the revised eIDAS Regulation (eIDAS 2.0). It is an emerging development. Member States must offer at least one wallet by 24 December 2026. Germany plans its state wallet, named "d-you", for 2 January 2027, with basic functions first. Regulated private sectors must accept the wallet about one year after issuance starts. Readiness varies widely between Member States. <!-- VERIFY: check current national timetables and acceptance obligations before publishing -->

For you as an earner, this means that the places where credentials can be kept will grow, but you should not assume that your badge can be loaded into a government wallet today. Ask your issuer which wallets and formats they support, and keep your own exported copy.

### Keeping your credentials in order

Make a short routine. Once a year, open your backpack and check three things: which credentials will expire in the next six months, whether any have been marked as revoked, and whether every issuer is still active. Export a fresh copy of your records after each check.

**Worked example: Jonas, learner at a training provider**

Jonas completes a vocational course at a training provider and receives a badge for "Spreadsheet analysis (Ability)". The provider recommends its own hosted backpack, but Jonas also wants a copy somewhere else. He claims the badge on the provider's platform, exports the credential file and saves it in a folder named "Credentials" on his own storage. He writes the issuer's name, the date and the badge link in a small list. Six months later the provider merges with another college and its platform is replaced. Jonas can still show the file and the issuer's details. When a recipient cannot verify the old badge page, he explains that the issuer has merged and offers the saved file and the college's contact for confirmation.

**Practice activity: Claim the demo badge**

Use the sandbox your facilitator provides. Follow these steps:

1. Open the demo notification email or the link given in the task.
2. Check the issuer name and read the badge details.
3. Claim the badge and store it in the backpack or wallet you chose.
4. Export or download a copy of the credential.
5. Write a three-line plan: what you would do if this credential expired, if it were revoked and if the issuer closed.

Keep your plan. It supports Outcome 4 and you can use it in your reflections.

## Sharing with purpose

### Decide why you share

Before you share a credential, ask a simple question: what do I want the recipient to understand? A recruiter needs different information from a tutor or a project partner. Sharing with purpose means you choose the credential, the channel and the amount of detail to fit that goal.

Common ways to share include:

- **A link** to the credential page in your backpack or on the issuer's platform.
- **An embedded badge or button** on a website or profile.
- **A profile entry** in a professional networking site, with the credential link.
- **A file** attached to a CV or application, if the process allows attachments.
- **A QR code** that points to the credential link, for printed material.

Each channel gives the recipient a different view. A link to a verification page shows the status in real time. A screenshot only shows an image and cannot be verified. Prefer a link when you can.

### Check how a recipient will see it

You can check the recipient view before you send anything. Open the shared link in a private browser window, or log out of your backpack and open it again. What you see is what a stranger sees. Look at these points:

- Is your name displayed as you want it?
- Are the issuer, criteria and date shown?
- Does the page show that the credential is valid or expired?
- Is any evidence attached, and are you happy for that to be public?
- Does the link work on a phone?

If the page shows more than you want, return to your settings. You will work on that in the module "Your privacy choices".

### Write a short, accurate description

When you add a credential to a profile or CV, you often write one or two sentences about it. Keep them accurate. Use the issuer's wording for the name, copy the criteria faithfully and state the date. If you want to add context, mark it as your own: "In this course I applied the method to a team rota."

Many people now draft profile text with an AI writing assistant. This can save time, but the tool does not know what your credential actually claims. It may add skills that sound plausible and that the credential never mentioned. Always compare the draft with the credential page:

1. Underline every claim in the draft: skills, levels, durations, results.
2. Find each claim in the credential's description or criteria.
3. Delete or reword anything that is not there.
4. Check that the issuer and date are correct.

Your name is on the profile, so you are responsible for every sentence.

**Worked example: Priya, learning designer at a university**

Priya is a learning designer at a university. She earns a badge "Designing accessible course materials (Ability)" from an association. She asks an AI writing assistant for a profile line and gets: "Expert in accessibility, certified to audit all university courses." She opens the badge page. She rewrites the line: "Completed the Ability badge 'Designing accessible course materials' (issued by the association, 4 November 2026): produced three accessible documents in a controlled exercise." ## Your privacy choices

### What a credential can reveal

A credential can show more than the badge name. It may display your full name, your email address, the date and place of the course, evidence you uploaded and the issuer's notes. Once a credential is public, search engines and other people can find it. You may want that for a professional profile and not for every credential.

Think of three groups of information:

- **Identity data:** name, email address, any identifier.
- **Achievement data:** badge name, criteria, issuer, date.
- **Supporting data:** evidence files, comments, endorsements.

Each group has a different risk. A public badge name can help recruiters find you. A public evidence file might contain a colleague's name or confidential data.

### The privacy dial

Most platforms offer three visibility levels. This course calls them the privacy dial:

- **Public:** anyone can find and view the credential, including through search.
- **Link-only:** only people who have the link can view it. It does not appear in search.
- **Private:** only you can see it.

Link-only is often a good default for credentials you send to a specific person. Public suits credentials you want to promote. Private suits credentials you keep for your own records or that you are not ready to share. Setting names and options differ between platforms, so check how your tool labels them and test the result.

### Make an informed choice

Use a short checklist for each credential:

1. **Who is the audience?** A public audience, one person or only you?
2. **What does the page show?** Check name, email, evidence and notes.
3. **Is there anything sensitive?** For example a colleague's name, a client or internal company data.
4. **How long should it stay visible?** Set a reminder to review.
5. **Can you change it later?** Most platforms allow this, but copies that others made may remain.

Remember that settings protect you only on the platform where you set them. If you post the credential on another site or send a file, the other site's rules apply.

## Using credentials in applications and conversations

### From notification to application

1. **Receive** the notification email and check the issuer.
2. **Claim** the credential and review its details.

5. **Share** the link in your application and check what the recipient will see.

### Choose which credentials to show

You do not have to share everything you hold. Match credentials to the role or purpose. A job advert, a course entry requirement or a mentoring request each calls for a different selection. Pick two or three credentials that are relevant and still valid. Put the strongest one first.

For each credential, prepare a one-line description that you can use in a CV, an application form or an interview. The line should contain the credential name, the issuer, the date and one sentence about what you did, in your own words, consistent with the criteria.

### Talk about your credentials

In an interview or conversation, a recipient may ask: "What did you do to earn this?" Use a short structure:

1. **What:** the name and the issuer.
2. **How:** the activity, taken from the criteria.
3. **Result:** what you produced, without exaggeration.
4. **Link:** where they can check it.

Be honest about the limits. A badge from a short workshop shows that you practised a task in a controlled setting. It does not show that you can do a full job. Say what you learned and how you would apply it, and let the recipient decide how much weight to give it.

### Handle questions about status

A recipient may find that a credential has expired or been revoked, or may not be able to open the link because the issuer has closed. Prepare for this.

- **Expired:** say so first. Explain the date and what you plan to do, such as taking a refresher.
- **Revoked:** do not present it as valid. If you believe the revocation is an error, contact the issuer and say that you are doing so.
- **Issuer closed:** explain what happened, offer your saved file and the issuer's last known details, and name another way the recipient can confirm your achievement, for example a referee.

Honesty protects you. A recipient who finds an unmentioned problem may doubt everything else you have said.

**Worked example: Sofia, applicant to a university programme**

Sofia applies to a university programme. The entry requirements ask for evidence of basic data skills. She holds a badge "Data basics (Ability)" from a training provider and a second, older badge whose issuer has closed. She chooses to submit the first one with a link-only page that she checked in a private window. In the application form she writes: "Data basics (Ability), issued by the training provider on 4 November 2026. I cleaned and summarised a small data set in a controlled exercise." She mentions the second badge in her cover letter: "The issuer has closed; I attach the saved file and the name of my tutor, who can confirm the course." The admissions team can follow the link, and Sofia has dealt with the status question before it arises.

## Assessment and badge

### The practical task

You complete a practical task in the sandbox. A person assesses your submission with the rubric below. The file does not grade your work.

**Task steps**

1. Claim the demo badge in the sandbox and store it in the backpack or wallet you chose.
2. Share the badge by link.
3. Set the visibility of the badge to the option that suits sharing it with one recipient, and check the recipient view in a private browser window.
4. Submit the link to the shared demo credential.
5. Write two short reflections of about 100 to 150 words each:
   - **Reflection 1:** Which visibility did you choose, what does a recipient see, and what did you decide to keep private? Explain your reasoning.
   - **Reflection 2:** Write the one-line description you would use in an application. Say which parts you checked against the credential, and what you changed if you used an AI assistant. Add what you would do if the credential expired, was revoked or the issuer closed.

**Evidence you submit for the badge:** the link to the shared demo credential and your two reflections.
